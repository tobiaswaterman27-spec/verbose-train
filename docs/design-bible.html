Wildhollow · design bible · draft two

The Wildhollow Register

A valley, a school, ten elements and one hundred Kith. Everything that has to be decided before a single sprite is worth drawing.

I

Premise

Mutation is supposed to take a lifetime. In Wildhollow it has started taking a season.

The valley sits over ten Wellsprings — places where a single element runs close enough to the surface to touch. The creatures of the valley, called Kith, change slowly and in step with the Wellspring nearest where they were born. That rhythm has held for as long as anyone has kept records. Three years ago it began to accelerate.

You arrive at Hollowbrook Academy as a first-year Binder. You are given one Kith, a blank register and a route. The work is ordinary at first: walk the valley, watch what lives in it, write down what you see. Then your class reaches the Verdant Wellspring for a routine sample, finds it drained to grey mud, and finds something living beside it wearing a shape the register has no name for.

II

The spine

Forced growth against growth in its own time.

Every system in the game is a version of that sentence. You cannot take a Kith, only be accepted by one — and you can rush that, and rushing costs you. A Kith changes when it has done the thing it is for — and there is a way to skip the doing, and it leaves the creature hollow. Headmaster Vosk found that way, and he found it out of kindness.

The design consequence is not negotiable: the player must be able to make Vosk's mistake themselves, at the scale of one animal, in the first hour. If the game has not let them force something and feel what came back, the ending is a lecture.

III

The three acts

Act I — Enrolment

Three Kith, ten students, one blank book

You choose one of three Kith and meet the nine classmates who carry the other lines. The tutorial is a syllabus — field practicals in Thistlemoor and Duskwood teach the element wheel because your tutor is teaching it, so instruction never has to stop the story. You learn to observe before you learn to fight: the first binding in the game is done entirely by watching, and the second one the game quietly lets you rush.

The turn. The class walks to the Verdant Wellspring for a sample every first-year takes, and finds grey mud and an animal with no entry.

Act II — The Survey

One region per element, one arc per classmate

The class is sent to survey all ten Wellsprings. Each is a region, a dungeon and a classmate's story at once, so the roster, the map and the cast advance on a single track. Four things accumulate: the Wellsprings are not failing but being tapped; whoever is tapping them has the Academy's permission; Ives Kalder disappears for a week and returns with a Kith three mutations ahead of where it should be and no account of how; and Ren Casimir, who has known for two years, starts leaving pages where you will find them.

The turn. You are offered the means to force a mutation yourself — plainly, without framing, at the exact point in the act where you most want it.

Act III — Forced Growth

The cost of not waiting

Vosk does not hide and does not deny it. He lost a Kith to a change that arrived too late to save it, and has spent twenty years making certain no student of his ever waits again. He is the best teacher in the building. He is emptying the valley to keep a promise he made to a dead animal.

The ending is one question with no good answer. Reseal the Wellsprings and every forced mutation in the valley reverts to the form it honestly earned — your classmates lose years and get back partners that can act on their own. Or leave them open, and the valley never stops accelerating: nothing settles, nothing can be catalogued, and the register becomes a record of a world that has already moved on.

You are a cataloguer. The game spends forty hours teaching you to love a knowable world, and then asks whether you have the right to keep it one.

IV

The ten elements

Each element has a Wellspring, and each Wellspring is a region. The map is the type chart — learning where things live is learning what beats what.

V

The valley

Ten regions ring the valley in the order of the element wheel, so the two things a new Binder has to learn — where things live, and what beats what — are the same lesson. Walk clockwise and you walk into what your Kith is strong against. Counter-clockwise, into what it fears.

VI

Hollowbrook Academy

Faculty

The class of ten

You take one of the three starter lines. The rest belong to the cast — each classmate's Kith is built to be theirs, not a slot on a selection screen.

VII

Binding

You cannot catch a Kith. You can only be accepted by one.

The entry is the mechanic

Every Kith in the valley has a register entry and every entry starts blank. Binding is finishing the entry. What you write in it — and how you came to know it — is the whole system.

Watched or pressed

Every observation is recorded one of two ways. You can learn a Flinch by leaving a Kith near a cold spring and watching it move away, or by hitting it with Frost until it flinches. Both fill the field. The entry records which.

The offer. With the entry full you may offer. The Kith weighs the page. Mostly watched and it comes with you. Mostly pressed and it refuses — and the next offer needs a cleaner page than the last.

Pressing is faster, and that is the point

A Binder in a hurry can fill an entry inside a single encounter. The game will let them. The Kith will not come. This is the first hour's lesson and the last hour's question, and it has to arrive in that order: force something small, lose it, and only then be shown a man who did the same thing to a valley.

VIII

Mutation

The Rite

A Kith does not change at a number. Every line carries a Rite — a condition of use rather than a level, and always something the creature already does. Sporelet's caps have to be shed and regrown. Cindercrow has to come off a thermal it should never have caught. A Binder who plays with a Kith honestly will meet its Rite without being told; one who reads it in a book still has to go and do it.

Branching

Most lines fork at the second form on how the Rite was met — early and hard down one path, late and careful down another. The same starter, played by two people, finishes the game as two different creatures.

Forcing

Wellspring water taken directly mutates a Kith in an afternoon. It works. The creature gets the form, the size and the numbers. What it does not get is whatever would have come from the doing — so a forced Kith never acts on its own initiative, and it stalls: now and then it loses a moment, standing in a body it has not grown into.

Forced Kith are stronger on paper and worse to have. That is not a punishment. It is the argument.

IX

Battle

Resolve, not health

A battle is not fought to unconsciousness. It is a contest of Resolve — how long a creature is willing to keep going. At zero a Kith breaks off. Yours comes back to you; a wild one leaves, and takes its unfinished entry with it.

Winning quickly is therefore often the wrong move. The encounter is where most observation happens, and a Kith you flatten in two turns is a page you did not finish.

Stance

Each turn you set a stance before choosing an action. Press hits harder and drops their Resolve faster, ending the encounter sooner than you may want. Hold gives ground, takes less, and keeps them in front of you. Read does not attack at all: it reveals their Tell and fills one field as watched.

Read is how the game is actually played, and it costs a turn every single time.

Initiative is the only thing Trust does

A Kith you have had a long time will occasionally act before you have told it to, and be right — sidestepping something it has seen before, moving on a Tell it read faster than you did. That is Trust. It is not a damage bonus. Your partner starts making decisions.

Forced Kith never do this. They wait to be told, every time, for the rest of the game.

X

The hundred

Three starters, not ten

An earlier draft offered all ten lines to the player. Three is the better number — a choice you can hold in your head — and it frees the other seven to belong to somebody. Ives Kalder's Umbra line can be built to be Ives's: a creature with a person attached, rather than a slot on a menu.

The standard

The first attempt at this roster produced eight creatures in an afternoon and seven were thrown away. They failed for the same three reasons every time: the body plan was one already on the roster, the element showed up as a colour instead of as a shape, and there was no answer to what the animal did when nobody was looking.

Nothing enters the register until all six of these are written down — in words, before any geometry is placed.

  1. A body plan no other Kith uses. Not a variation. A different animal.
  2. A living. What it eats, where it sleeps, what it is afraid of.
  3. Its element in its shape. Colour is the last five per cent, never the first.
  4. Three observations a Binder could actually make. If you cannot say what watching it would teach you, it is not finished — and it cannot be bound, because binding runs on exactly this.
  5. A Rite that is the thing it already does. Not a level, not an item.
  6. One fact with no gameplay purpose whatsoever.

And one rule about process: one at a time. The seven that were cut were designed in a batch, and a batch converges — the second borrows from the first, and the eighth is a recolour of the third.

XI

Built so far

Three of one hundred. Sprite models exist and validate in all four views. Entries written to the standard above, as the worked example of it.

Wildhollow · draft two · names, elements, regions, systems and cast are original to this project. Ninety-seven entries remain, and will be written one at a time.
